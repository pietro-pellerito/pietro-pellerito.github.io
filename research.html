Job Market Paper

"Breaking Boundaries: Competitive Effects of Inter-District School Choice"

Abstract: Inter-district school choice (IDSC) allows students to attend non-residential public schools, leading to enrollment pressure that may incentivize school districts to improve educational quality. I test this claim by exploiting an unanticipated Indiana funding reform eliminating transfer tuition, sharply reducing household costs to utilize IDSC. Using a statewide student-level administrative dataset, I estimate a continuous difference-in-differences model where school districts' enrollment-threat exposure is constructed from pre-policy achievement gaps with neighboring districts. Districts competing against higher-achieving neighbors face greater potential enrollment losses after transfer tuition is eliminated. This exposure predicts realized enrollment losses: a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure reduces enrollment through net transfers by 2.06 percent. Consistent with districts responding to this pressure by improving academic quality, the same exposure increase narrows achievement gaps with neighboring districts by 5.3 percent, an 8.4 percent reduction for elementary grades. Competitive effects are larger in smaller, geographically compact districts, where losses are harder to absorb and alternatives are more accessible. These findings identify enrollment incentives as a channel through which reducing barriers to IDSC can reduce educational inequality.

Working Papers

"Staying Connected: Experimental Effects of Weekly Text Messages in an Adult High School" – with Patrick Turner and Alberto Ortega

Abstract: More than 23 million adults in the United States lack a high school diploma, and most who return to school drop out before graduating. We evaluate a randomized intervention of adult high school students in which coaches delivered text messages that encouraged persistence. We find that assignment to treatment increased graduation by 4.9 percentage points, and the effect of message receipt among compliers is 9.3 percentage points, a 41 percent increase relative to the control mean. The intervention operated on the intensive margin by increasing the number of courses completed, but did not affect student enrollment. The effects of the intervention are concentrated among students assigned to experienced coaches, who customized their messages more extensively. We contextualize the economic impact of the low-cost intervention by comparing labor market earnings of graduates to non-graduates, which implies an earnings benefit of $149 per treated student over four years.
