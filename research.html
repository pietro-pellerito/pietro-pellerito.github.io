Competition and School District Behavior: Evidence from Inter-District Open Enrollment

Abstract

School choice policies have dramatically expanded in the United States over the past few decades. Yet, there is little direct evidence on how public school districts respond to competition. I use a new identification strategy to estimate the impacts of exposure to inter-district open enrollment competition on the traditional public school districts in Michigan. I leverage both rule-based limitations and frictional distance associated with cross-district enrollment to develop instruments that capture the intensity of competitive pressure as it varies over time, across grades, and across districts. I find evidence of systematic responses, particularly in revenue generation, with districts raising additional local revenue and reducing reliance on the state funding formula in response to competitive pressure. The effect on total expenditure is negative and only marginally significant, but there are large and economically meaningful reallocations in spending toward instruction and away from auxiliary services. These patterns are stronger among higher-capacity districts, indicating heterogeneity in districts' ability to respond. There is limited evidence of improvements in student outcomes or changes in enrollment and composition at the district level, suggesting that responses occur primarily through fiscal and expenditure channels.
