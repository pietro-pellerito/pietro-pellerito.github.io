Job Market Paper

"Breaking Boundaries: Competitive Effects of Inter-District School Choice"

Abstract: Inter-district school choice (IDSC), which allows students to attend public schools outside their residential districts, ' may improve educational quality by exposing districts to enrollment pressure. Yet district responses to expanded access to alternative public schools remain understudied. I exploit an unanticipated Indiana funding reform that eliminated transfer tuition in 2010, sharply reducing household participation costs. Using statewide student-level administrative data, I estimate a continuous difference-in-differences model using districts’ IDSC enrollment-threat exposure, constructed from pre-policy achievement gaps with neighboring districts; districts competing against higher-achieving neighbors face greater enrollment pressure after transfer tuition is eliminated. A one-standard-deviation increase in exposure produces a net enrollment loss through transfers equal to 2.06 percent of district enrollment. The same increase raises district value added, a measure of academic quality, by 0.0074 test-score standard deviations, reducing the initial achievement gap between lower- and higher-exposure districts by 5.3 percent. Gains are concentrated in elementary grades, where the effect reaches 0.0156 standard deviations. Quality rises immediately, while IDSC participation and enrollment shifts develop gradually, consistent with responses to anticipated rather than realized losses. I find no evidence that observed student composition or measured resources explain the quality response. Consistent with enrollment threats, elementary-grade effects are larger in smaller, geographically compact districts, where losses are harder to absorb and alternatives more accessible. These findings identify enrollment incentives as a channel through which reducing IDSC barriers can improve educational quality.
